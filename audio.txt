Stu, good morning. Today, more than 50 House GOP members led by Congressman Mike Kelly and Gus
Bilirakis sent a letter to the Trump administration urging them to block Chinese companies from
building vehicle and battery manufacturing plants in the U.S. They're concerned about
national security, and they write, Chinese vehicles include advanced technology systems
capable of aggregating and transmitting sensitive data from U.S. citizens and companies that could
be accessed and weaponized by the Chinese government. And just last week, a pair of
bipartisan senators, Bernie Moreno and Alyssa Slotkin, introduced a bill that would ban the
import, sale, and operation of vehicles manufactured in China or any other country of concern.
But here's the thing, Stuart. These vehicles are already driving on U.S. roads. Chinese car
brands BYD, Geely, and Great Wall Motor have dealerships in Mexico. We've seen reports that
those vehicles are regularly spotted on the streets of El Paso, Texas after being purchased in Mexico,
then driven over the border. The industry group that represents automakers in the U.S. points out
that the Chinese government offers massive subsidies for its automakers.
China has a massive automotive overcapacity problem. They make about 20 million more vehicles
in production in China than their market can consume. And so the Chinese government wants to support and
encourage and subsidize the exportation of those vehicles around the world.
So you might be wondering, how do these vehicles drive? Well, the folks at Edmunds who test drive
hundreds of vehicles a year, they got their hands on a Geely Galaxy M9. They set out to answer whether
that Chinese-made plug-in hybrid SUV could compete in America if it were sold here. And the answer they
came to is yes. The vehicle that we tested, if you just do a straight conversion, would be about
$35,000. Now, clearly we wouldn't expect to see one on sale in the U.S. for that price. But even if
it was $50,000, it would still be competitive against the Hyundais, the Fords, the GMs, and
everything else that's currently on sale. But lawmakers say that the playing field just isn't
level when you have the Chinese government investing in automaking in that country. And that is a huge
challenge for U.S. manufacturers if those vehicles were allowed to be sold in the U.S. market.
